[The Amateur Entomologists' Society's annual convention](https://www.amentsoc.org/events/exhibitions.html) was recommended to me by a reptile store employee I'd spoken to the week prior. 
The following Saturday morning, my partner and I took a train to Kempton Park Racecourse, Surrey, and found our way to the venue.

We emerged into the convention's main hall, busy with entomologists with their wooden boxes, and surrounded by a musty smell which I assume came from the pinned insects and live invertebrates.

For the first hour, we explored the convention, gawking at the variety of beetles, moths, butterflies and tarantulas pinned and organised for their selection by hobbyists. 
Further in, we found the live millipedes, centipedes and the prior mentioned invertebrates also for sale as live pets.

![Image](/images/millipedes.png){: width="300" }
*Box of millipedes, including one that I held*

We spent some time with [Isolife's stand](https://isolife.co.uk/) as my partner was looking for isopods for her vampire crab paludarium.
The employees were lovely and directed us to their starter kit and a small colony of *Cubaris m. 'Mandarin'* which we bought planning to breed in preparation for supplying the future vampire crabs with a cleaning crew.

![Image](/images/isopod.png){: width="120"}
*One of our new Cubaris m. 'Mandarin'*

I also considered buying one of their millipedes and went to [Venomous Visions' stand](https://www.venomousvisions.co.uk/) to practice handling one to see if I liked it. I almost bought one, but decided it would be best to wait and get a feel for the isopods before embarking on a life of creepy crawlies. 

The next item on the agenda was finding a pinned Atlas moth as a birthday gift for a party the next day.
These are one of the largest insects on Earth, with a maximum wingspan of 30cm, found in South and Southeast Asia.

Whilst looking for one, we spoke to an entomologist at smaller stand, Andrew Spicer, who told us about his discovery in Cameroon a few years back.
It is was previously understood that _Eudaemonia_ do not have mouthparts (proboscis), the _Eudaemonia argiphontes_ was included in this assumption.
On a entomology trip with his son, he noticed this species drinking water from his hand, proving this to be incorrect. 
Later in the year, they also ["observed the _Eudaemonia argiphontes_ would die overnight in captivity unless they were housed in a cage with available surface water"](https://www.cameroonark.org/tales-of-tongues).

Moving on, we found a few sellers with the Atlas moth and opted for the cheapest from the smaller, but very friendly, stand run by [Bob Dollery](https://www.amentsoc.org/about/news/0257/) upstairs.

![Image](/images/atlasMoth.png){: width="260"}
*Behold, the Atlas moth*

The convention had a warm and enthusiastic atmosphere, and everyone we spoke to was simply excited to talk about that which they loved: invertebrates. I hope to go again next year if possible, although I doubt I'll have the same grit to come home bugless.

## Back home
After the train trip back to my flat, we laid out the contents of the starter kit, keen to get the *Cubaris m. 'Mandarin'* out of their little plastic tub into their new home.

![Image](/images/kitContents.jpeg){: width="400"}
*Contents of isopod kit*

We placed the substrates, added the dried leaves and the obstacles, moss and consumables.
To those who know what they're doing, don't worry, we noticed that we'd missed setting up a moisture gradient and corrected the layout later on.
